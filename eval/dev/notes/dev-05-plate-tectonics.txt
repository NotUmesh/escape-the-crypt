Plate tectonics

Earth's outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into large slabs called tectonic plates. The lithosphere includes the crust and the rigid top layer of the mantle. The plates float on the asthenosphere, a hotter, softer layer of the mantle that can flow very slowly.

Plates move only a few centimetres per year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow. Over millions of years, this slow movement reshapes continents and oceans. About 200 million years ago, the continents were joined in a single supercontinent called Pangaea, which then broke apart.

In 1912, Alfred Wegener proposed the idea of continental drift. He pointed to matching coastlines and matching fossils on continents that are now far apart. Most scientists rejected his idea at first because he could not explain what force moved the continents.

Scientists group plate boundaries into three types. At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and new crust forms as magma rises; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is an example. At convergent boundaries, plates move towards each other; the Himalayas formed where the Indian Plate collided with the Eurasian Plate. At transform boundaries, plates slide past each other sideways; the San Andreas Fault in California is an example. A hotspot is not a type of plate boundary: it is a plume of hot mantle rising under the middle of a plate, as under Hawaii.

When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, subduction follows a typical order. First, the denser oceanic plate bends downward beneath the continental plate. Next, the sinking plate heats up as it descends into the mantle. Then water released from the sinking plate helps nearby mantle rock melt into magma. Finally, the magma rises and can erupt to form a line of volcanoes on the continent.

Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate boundaries. The Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean is a belt of frequent earthquakes and volcanoes caused by subduction.
