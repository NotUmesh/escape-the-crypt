Supply and demand

The law of demand says that, other things being equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls. The law of supply says that when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises, because producers can earn more by selling more.

The market equilibrium is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. At this price there is no pressure for the price to change.

If the price is set below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer, which creates a shortage. If the price is set above equilibrium, sellers offer more than buyers want, which creates a surplus.

In one worked example from class, the price of a cup of coffee rose from 2 dollars to 3 dollars, and the quantity demanded at a cafe fell from 100 cups a day to 80 cups a day.

A change in price moves the market along the demand curve. A change in anything else that affects buyers shifts the whole curve. These notes list three demand shifters: income, consumer preferences and the prices of related goods. The cost of producing the good is not a demand shifter; it affects supply.

Related goods come in two kinds. Substitutes are goods used in place of each other, such as tea and coffee: when coffee becomes more expensive, demand for tea rises. Complements are goods used together, such as printers and ink cartridges: when printers become cheaper, demand for ink cartridges rises.

Governments sometimes control prices. A price ceiling is a legal maximum price, such as rent control; if it is set below equilibrium, it causes a shortage. A price floor is a legal minimum price, such as a minimum wage; if it is set above equilibrium, it causes a surplus.

Prices act as signals. A rising price tells producers to make more and tells buyers to look for alternatives, which helps markets move back towards equilibrium.
