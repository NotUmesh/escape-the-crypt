How blood clots

Blood is made of four main components. Plasma is the pale yellow liquid part and makes up about 55 percent of blood volume. Red blood cells carry oxygen. White blood cells fight infection. Platelets are small cell fragments that help blood clot. Haemoglobin is not a separate component of blood: it is the protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen.

When a blood vessel is cut, the body stops the bleeding in a fixed order called haemostasis. First, the damaged vessel narrows, which is called vascular spasm, and this reduces blood flow. Next, platelets stick to the damaged wall and to each other, forming a loose platelet plug. Then clotting factors in the plasma work together to turn fibrinogen into fibrin, which forms a mesh of threads that traps blood cells. Finally, the clot tightens and the vessel wall is repaired, and the clot is later broken down.

Different cells last for different lengths of time. Red blood cells live for about 120 days. Platelets live for only about 7 to 10 days, so the body makes new ones all the time.

A normal platelet count is between 150,000 and 450,000 platelets per microlitre of blood.

The liver needs vitamin K to make several clotting factors. Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which the blood does not clot normally because a clotting factor is missing or does not work properly.
