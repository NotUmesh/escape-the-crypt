Apollo 11: the first crewed Moon landing

Apollo 11 was the NASA mission that first landed humans on the Moon. It launched from Kennedy Space Center in Florida on July 16, 1969, on a Saturn V rocket.

The crew had three astronauts. Neil Armstrong was the commander. Buzz Aldrin was the lunar module pilot, who flew down to the surface with Armstrong. Michael Collins was the command module pilot, who stayed in lunar orbit while the other two landed. Yuri Gagarin was not part of Apollo 11: he was a Soviet cosmonaut who became the first human in space in 1961.

The spacecraft had two main parts that separated near the Moon. The command module, named Columbia, carried the crew to the Moon and back to Earth. The lunar module, named Eagle, carried two astronauts from lunar orbit down to the surface and back up again.

The mission followed a planned sequence. First, the Saturn V launched the crew into Earth orbit and then sent them towards the Moon. Next, the spacecraft entered orbit around the Moon on July 19. Then Eagle separated from Columbia and landed in the Sea of Tranquility on July 20. After the moonwalk, Eagle lifted off and docked with Columbia again. Finally, Columbia returned to Earth and splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24.

Armstrong and Aldrin spent about 21 and a half hours on the lunar surface, but only about two and a half hours of that time walking outside. During the moonwalk they collected about 21.5 kilograms of rock and soil, set up experiments and planted a United States flag.

One experiment left behind was a laser reflector, a panel of mirrors that scientists on Earth still use to measure the distance to the Moon by timing how long a laser pulse takes to bounce back.

Apollo 11 met the goal set by President John F. Kennedy in 1961 of landing a person on the Moon and returning them safely to Earth before the end of the decade.
