The life cycle of a butterfly

Butterflies go through complete metamorphosis, which has four stages. First, an adult female lays an egg, usually on a plant the young will eat. Next, a larva, called a caterpillar, hatches and spends most of its time eating and growing. Then the caterpillar forms a pupa, which in butterflies is called a chrysalis, and its body is rebuilt inside. Finally, an adult butterfly emerges, ready to find a mate and start the cycle again.

Each stage has a different main job. The egg protects the developing embryo. The caterpillar's job is to eat and store energy. The pupa is where the body is reorganised into the adult form. The adult's job is to reproduce and spread to new places.

A caterpillar grows so quickly that it must shed its skin several times, a process called moulting. Most caterpillars moult about five times before they become a pupa.

Insects that go through complete metamorphosis covered in these notes include butterflies, beetles, flies and bees. Grasshoppers do not: they go through incomplete metamorphosis, with only three stages, egg, nymph and adult, and the young look like small adults.

Some monarch butterflies migrate up to about 4,800 kilometres between Canada and Mexico each year.
