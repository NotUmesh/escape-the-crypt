The water cycle

The water cycle describes how water moves continuously between the oceans, the air and the land. Because it is a cycle, it has no true starting point: water can enter it at any stage and keep circulating.

About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans. Only about 3 percent is fresh water, and most of that fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps rather than flowing in rivers and lakes.

Evaporation happens when the Sun heats liquid water and turns it into water vapour, a gas. Most evaporation takes place over the oceans. Transpiration is the release of water vapour from the leaves of plants. Sublimation is when ice turns directly into water vapour without melting first, which happens on cold, dry, sunny mountain slopes.

Condensation is the opposite of evaporation: water vapour cools and turns back into tiny liquid droplets. Clouds form when billions of these droplets gather around specks of dust in the air. A single molecule of water stays in the atmosphere for about nine days on average before it falls again.

Precipitation is any water that falls from clouds to the ground, including rain, snow, sleet and hail. Fog and dew are not precipitation, because they form at ground level instead of falling from clouds.

Once water reaches the land, it can take several paths. Runoff is water that flows over the surface into streams, rivers and eventually the sea. Infiltration is water soaking down into the soil. Water that soaks deep enough to fill the spaces in rock becomes groundwater, which can stay underground for thousands of years.

Human activity changes parts of the cycle. Paving over land reduces infiltration and increases runoff, which is one reason cities can flood quickly after heavy rain.
