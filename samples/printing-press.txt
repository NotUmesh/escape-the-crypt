The printing press

By about 1450, Johannes Gutenberg had developed a printing press in Mainz, in what is now Germany, after early experiments in Strasbourg. Before this, books in Europe were usually copied by hand, which was slow and expensive.

Gutenberg combined three main innovations. He made movable type from metal, so individual letters could be cast in large numbers and reused. He used an oil-based ink that stuck well to metal type. He adapted a screw press, similar to those used for pressing grapes, to push paper evenly onto the inked type. Paper was not one of his inventions: it had been invented in China centuries earlier and reached Europe through the Islamic world.

Movable type itself was older than Gutenberg. In China, Bi Sheng made movable type from baked clay around 1040. In Korea, the book known as Jikji was printed with movable metal type in 1377. Gutenberg's achievement was a complete system that made printing fast and cheap in Europe.

Printing a page followed a set order. First, the printer arranged individual letters in a composing stick to form lines of text. Next, the lines were locked into a frame called a forme. Then the type was inked using leather ink balls. After that, a sheet of damp paper was laid on the type and pressed with the screw press. Finally, the printed sheet was hung up to dry.

The Gutenberg Bible, finished around 1455, has 42 lines on most pages. About 180 copies were printed.

Printing spread quickly across Europe. It made books cheaper, helped ideas travel faster and made it easier for more people to learn to read.
