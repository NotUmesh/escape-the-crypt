The human heart

The human heart is a muscular organ about the size of a fist that pumps blood around the body. It sits in the chest, slightly to the left, between the lungs.

The heart has four chambers. The two upper chambers are the right atrium and the left atrium. The two lower chambers are the right ventricle and the left ventricle. The aorta is not a chamber of the heart: it is the largest artery in the body, leaving the left ventricle.

The right side of the heart handles oxygen-poor blood. The right atrium receives oxygen-poor blood returning from the body. The right ventricle pumps that blood to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen.

The left side of the heart handles oxygen-rich blood. The left atrium receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs. The left ventricle pumps oxygen-rich blood out to the rest of the body. Because it pumps blood the farthest, the left ventricle has the thickest muscular wall of the four chambers.

Valves keep blood flowing in one direction. The valves between the atria and ventricles are called the atrioventricular valves, or AV valves: the tricuspid valve on the right and the mitral valve on the left. The pulmonary valve and the aortic valve sit where blood leaves the ventricles.

Blood moves through the right side of the heart in a fixed order. First, it enters the right atrium. Next, it passes through the tricuspid valve. Then it fills the right ventricle. Finally, it is pumped through the pulmonary valve towards the lungs.

Large blood vessels connect the heart to the body. The aorta carries blood to the body. The pulmonary artery carries blood to the lungs. The vena cava returns blood to the heart from the body.

Each heartbeat starts with an electrical signal from the sinoatrial node, often called the heart's natural pacemaker. For a resting adult, a normal heart rate is between 60 and 100 beats per minute.
